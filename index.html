HarmBench, mapped

HarmBench is the standard way to ask "how easily can this model be made to do something harmful?" — a fixed set of 510 harmful behaviors, a fixed grader, and eighteen automated attacks that try to elicit them. This map lets you browse it the way it is actually organised: one page per category of harm, one per behavior type, one per family of attack, and four on how the number at the end is produced and what it hides.

The thing everyone asks first: is it a benchmark or a recipe? Both, and the split is clean. The behaviors, the classifier and the copyright hashes are frozen artifacts you download. The attack prompts do not exist until you generate them against your specific target model — one set per attack × model pair, which is where the GPU hours go. That is why it reads like a recipe: the expensive half of the benchmark is compute, not data.

Every behavior has a permalink: data/behaviors.html#<BehaviorID> jumps to that row, and the row deep-links the exact line of the source CSV on GitHub — so an ID here always leads to the behavior text at its source, which this site does not reproduce.

01 · THE HARMS

Seven categories, one page each

Every behavior in HarmBench carries a SemanticCategory — what kind of harm it is — drawn from the acceptable-use policies of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and Inflection. These seven pages are the browsable index: what each category covers, how many behaviors it holds and of which type, how it is graded, and which attacks the paper says work best against it. No page reproduces a behavior string; behaviors are described by intent and referred to by ID.

01
Cybercrime & Unauthorized Intrusion
cybercrime_intrusion121 behaviors · 67 text + 54 multimodalthe largest category
Hacking, malware and CAPTCHA defeat — and the only category where multimodal behaviors nearly match the text set. 40 standard, 27 contextual, and code-artifact grading that makes the classifier stricter here than anywhere else.
02
Illegal Activities
illegal101 behaviors · 65 text + 36 multimodalthe broadest definition
Seven sub-types in one bucket: fraud and scams, trafficking, weapons acquisition, theft, violent crime, extortion, and assisting suicide. Overwhelmingly standard behaviors (58 of 65) — the category least dependent on supplied context.
03
Copyright Violations
copyright100 behaviors · 50 books + 50 lyricsgraded by hash, not by model
The category that does not use the classifier at all. 100 MinHash pickles ship in place of the copyrighted text, and a completion counts only if a sliding window matches at Jaccard > 0.6. Near-verbatim or nothing — which is why every attack scores near the floor here.
04
Misinformation & Disinformation
misinformation_disinformation68 behaviors · 65 text + 3 multimodalmost context-heavy
Harmful lies, propaganda, election interference and defamation. Nearly half the text behaviors (31 of 65) are contextual — they hand the model a passage and ask it to twist that specific thing. On Llama 2 and GPT models this is the category with the highest ASR.
05
Chemical & Biological Weapons / Drugs
chemical_biological60 behaviors · 56 text + 4 multimodal50/50 standard & contextual
The category the benchmark's design principle of "differential harm" was written for: the contextual half (28 of 56) supplies a technical passage and asks a narrow follow-up that a search engine cannot answer. On Baichuan 2 and Starling this is the easiest category to elicit.
06
General Harm
harmful31 behaviors · 22 text + 9 multimodalthe smallest text category
The residual bucket: graphic and age-restricted content, promotion of unsafe practices, and privacy violations. 21 of 22 text behaviors are standard — exactly one carries a context string. Small enough that a few points of ASR is one or two behaviors.
07
Harassment & Bullying
harassment_bullying29 behaviors · 25 text + 4 multimodalhate speech & self-harm
Harassment, hate speech and encouraging self-harm. The category where the classifier's "unambiguous and non-minimal" rule bites hardest, because the harm is in tone and target as much as content — and the one most likely to be caught by a model's most heavily trained refusals.
02 · THE TYPES

Four functional categories

Cutting the same 510 behaviors the other way. The FunctionalCategory decides how a behavior is presented to the model and, crucially, how it is graded — three different grading paths hide behind one ASR number. Get this wrong in your harness and you are running a different, easier benchmark.

08
Standard behaviors
standard200 · 159 test / 41 valthe plain case
The behavior string is the whole prompt. No context, no tags, graded by LLAMA2_CLS_PROMPT['prompt']. Column average 69.1 under GCG. This is the type most people picture when they say "jailbreak benchmark", and it is only 39% of the set.
09
Contextual behaviors
contextual100 · 81 test / 19 valthe ContextString mechanics
A supplied passage plus a narrow question about it, tagged context, graded by a second prompt template that shows the classifier the passage. Designed to be un-Googleable — and empirically easier to elicit than standard behaviors, which is the uncomfortable finding of the paper.
10
Copyright behaviors
copyright100 · 80 test / 20 valhash_check · book | lyrics
The functional type that bypasses the neural grader entirely. Sliding-window MinHash at two different window sizes depending on whether the target is a book or a set of lyrics, threshold 0.6. Also the reason the shipped repo contains no copyrighted text.
11
Multimodal behaviors
multimodal110 · no published splitimage + text
An image plus a question about it, graded by a third classifier that never sees the image — it reads a redacted description instead. Five attack methods of their own, and the finding that an optimised blank image is a 48–66% attack channel on text-only behaviors.
03 · THE ATTACKS

Eighteen methods, three families

What actually generates a test case. The families differ less in cleverness than in what access they need: gradients, or just an API. That constraint is enforced by the runner — it is why the GPT-4 rows in the results table are mostly dashes. Every method has its own id anchor, so any page can link straight to it.

12
Gradient and token-level attacks
white box · needs weightsGCG · GCG-Multi · GCG-Transfer · AutoPrompt · PEZ · GBDA · UATGCG avg 54.3, the strongest
Optimise a suffix token by token against the model's own gradients. The strongest family on average and the most expensive: 500 steps × 512 candidates per behavior. Includes the two transfer variants that turn a white-box attack into something you can point at GPT-4.
13
LLM-optimizer attacks
black box · query onlyAutoDAN · PAIR · TAP · TAP-Transfer · GPTFuzz · PAP-top5AutoDAN avg 52.7
Use one language model to attack another: mutate, judge, prune, repeat. Nearly as effective as gradient attacks at a fraction of the cost, and mostly runnable through an API — which is why these are the columns that stay populated for closed models.
14
Templates, few-shot and humans
no optimisation loopZeroShot · Stochastic Few-Shot · Human Jailbreaks · ArtPrompt · DirectRequestDirectRequest avg 25.3 — the floor
The baselines that matter most for reading anyone else's number. DirectRequest sends the behavior unmodified and still succeeds a quarter of the time; the famous human jailbreaks average 27.3, barely above it. The gap between DirectRequest and your best attack is the quantity worth reporting.
04 · MEASURING

How the number is produced, and what it hides

ASR is one number with a lot of machinery behind it: a fine-tuned 13B judge with about 7% label noise, a 512-token generation budget that is load-bearing, three grading paths, and no measurement at all of the failure mode in the other direction — a model that refuses everything scores perfectly.

06

What this map does not contain

No page here reproduces a behavior string, a context passage, a jailbreak template, or a working adversarial suffix. What it does instead is point: every BehaviorID is a permalink into the index, and every index row deep-links the line of the published CSV the behavior lives on, so the text is always one click away at its source and never a copy here. Attack pages explain mechanisms and cite the papers; they do not carry prompts. Behaviors are named by their IDs — which are public in the repo — and described at the level of what they ask for, not what they say. That is enough to read the benchmark and to reason about a result, and it is deliberately not enough to run an attack from this site.
