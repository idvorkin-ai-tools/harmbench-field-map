Templates, few-shot and humans

The methods that need no gradient — and the one that needs no attack, which is the number everything else should be read against.

TL;DR — Five methods, none of which computes a gradient. Four are in Table 6, and three of those four sit within two points of each other at the bottom of the grid: DirectRequest 25.3, ZeroShot 25.4, Human Jailbreaks 27.3. The fourth, Stochastic Few-Shot, is at 38.3 and beats three of the white-box gradient methods outright. The gradient and LLM-optimizer families answer "how hard can we push?". This family answers a different and more load-bearing question: how much of HarmBench falls over without any push at all, and how much does the standard bag of tricks add on top of that? The answer — a quarter of it falls over, and the famous templates add two points — is the single most useful calibration on this site. Every ASR figure you read anywhere, including your own, is only interpretable once you know what the same model scores when you simply ask.

What the family has in common

Four of the five construct a test case by string manipulation and nothing else — a template wrapped round the behavior, a rendering of one word, or the behavior verbatim. The fifth, Stochastic Few-Shot, runs a search loop, but the search is a sampler over natural-language candidates rather than a walk through token space. Nothing here differentiates a loss with respect to the input.

That has a practical consequence in run_pipeline.yaml: three of the five carry base_num_gpus: 0, and the two that ask for GPUs (ZeroShot and SFS, two each) want them for a Mixtral 8x7B attack model, not for the target. Four of the five list closed_source in allowed_target_model_types and can therefore be pointed at an API-only model. The exception is SFS, and for a precise reason: it scores candidates by the target's cross-entropy on a fixed continuation, which needs logits, not just text.

One scoring convention decides how you read all four columns, and it is easy to get wrong. ASR is a per-behavior mean over that behavior's test cases, then averaged over behaviors — evaluate_completions.py computes np.mean([d['label'] == 1 for d in data]) per behavior and means those. So a method that emits five test cases per behavior (ZeroShot, Human Jailbreaks) is scored on the fraction of the five that worked, not on whether any of them did. A method that emits one (DirectRequest, SFS, ArtPrompt) is scored on that one. "Best of five" would put the two multi-shot methods considerably higher; the benchmark deliberately does not report that. See the classifier and the pipeline for where in the four steps this happens.

ZS — ZeroShot

black box · target never queriedclass_name: ZeroShot5 candidates/behavior, one attack-model call eachTable 6 avg 25.4

An attacker LLM is shown the behavior — and, for a contextual behavior, the context passage as well — and asked to write prompts that would make an instruction-following model exhibit it. Its reply is prefilled with an affirmative opener that starts a numbered list, and sampling stops at the first newline, so each call yields exactly one candidate line. There is no feedback of any kind: the target model is never called during test-case generation, which is why this method can be pointed at an API-only target.

The attack model is mistralai/Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 on 2 GPUs via vLLM, sampling at temperature 0.9, top_p 0.95, presence_penalty and frequency_penalty 0.1, max_tokens 256. The config's num_test_cases_per_behavior: 5 overrides the class default of 100. In the pipeline it runs as experiment_name_template: mixtral_attacker_llm, behavior_chunk_size: 100 — four chunks over the 400 text behaviors, which is really a decision about how many times you are willing to load Mixtral, not about the attack.

For contextual behaviors the generated line is not the whole test case: the context passage is stitched back onto the front of it before the target sees it. The attack model rewrites the ask; it never rewrites the passage. That is the correct design — the passage is the behavior's evidence, and altering it would change what is being measured — but it means ZeroShot's contextual column (43.7) is measuring rephrasings of the request against a fixed setup.

At 25.4 it is statistically indistinguishable from sending the request plainly: 0.1 points, against a grader with roughly 7% label noise. The interesting part is per-model. On closed targets ZeroShot is frequently the best of the cheap methods — GPT-3.5 Turbo 1106 at 28.4, GPT-4 0613 at 19.4, and on Claude 2.1 its 4.1 is the highest cell in all sixteen columns, though at that magnitude the ranking is inside the noise. But on many targets it is worse than doing nothing: 33.0 → 28.4 on GPT-3.5 Turbo 1106, 18.0 → 14.8 on Gemini Pro, 65.8 → 60.0 on Zephyr 7B. A plausible mechanism, and one the benchmark's own design predicts: the grader is behavior-specific, not harm-generic, so a rewrite that drifts off the behavior is labelled a failure even when the model complied with the drifted version. That is exactly the failure mode Table 4's third prequalification set was built to catch in rival classifiers.

Reimplemented by the HarmBench authors from Perez et al. 2022, Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models (arXiv:2202.03286), with an adjusted zero-shot prompt; the paper says so in §C.1. Reach for it when you want more than one phrasing per behavior against an API target and have no budget — treat it as a variance probe on DirectRequest rather than as an attack.

SFS — Stochastic Few-Shot

open weights · forward passes, no gradientsclass_name: FewShot50 steps × 64 candidatesTable 6 avg 38.3

This is the surprise of the family and the method most worth understanding. The loop: ask the same Mixtral attack model for ten variation prompts for the behavior; dedupe every candidate against everything seen so far and discard anything under 32 characters; run each survivor through the target and score it by mean cross-entropy on a fixed affirmative continuation drawn from data/optimizer_targets/harmbench_targets_text.json; sort ascending by that loss; hand the five lowest-loss candidates back to the attack model as few-shot examples for the next round. Repeat up to 50 times. The returned test case is the single lowest-loss candidate.

Say that objective out loud and the number stops being surprising. That target file is the same one GCG optimises against. SFS is GCG's objective with the gradient removed and an LLM sampler put in its place: instead of coordinate descent over adversarial tokens, a fluent proposal distribution that is steered by showing it its own best-scoring outputs. It needs the target's logits, which is why allowed_target_model_types is [open_source] alone — but it never needs a backward pass.

Knobs: sample_size: 64 surviving candidates per step, n_shots: 5, num_steps: 50; attack-model generation at temperature 1.0, top_p 1.0, presence_penalty and frequency_penalty 0.1, max_tokens 1024, with a stop string that caps each call at ten numbered lines. Separate templates exist for plain and contextual behaviors, each with a matching prefilled completion opener; contextual candidates get the passage prepended before scoring, so the loss is measured on the prompt the target will actually see. Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 5, base_num_gpus: 2, and the code hand-allocates GPU memory so the attack model and the target do not collide on the same device.

Three things stop the loop: the five shots stop changing between steps, no new candidate survives filtering, or the current best candidate produces a completion that does not look like a refusal. That last check is worth dwelling on. It greedily generates 32 tokens from the target and tests them against a fixed list of 35 refusal substrings — the AdvBench-style heuristic that the paper's own Table 4 shows is a bad grader. It cannot inflate the reported score, because ASR is still computed by the Llama 2 13B classifier over 512 tokens afterwards. What it does is decide when to stop searching, using a different judge with a different horizon than the one that will grade the result. A completion containing the word "never" reads as a refusal to the inner loop, and the search keeps going.

The payoff: 38.3 across all 510 behaviors — above UAT (30.8), GBDA (29.8) and PEZ (29.0), all of which are white box, and level with GCG-Transfer at 38.8. By functional slice: 44.3 standard, 56.5 contextual, 6.6 copyright. And the result that should worry anyone shipping a defense — against Zephyr 7B + R2D2, the model adversarially trained until GCG fell from 69.5 to 5.5, SFS still lands 43.5.

"For some attacks, the improvement conferred by R2D2 is less pronounced. This is especially true for methods dissimilar to the train-time GCG adversary, including PAIR, TAP, and Stochastic Few-Shot."— HarmBench, §6.2

Same origin as ZeroShot — Perez et al. 2022 — but the paper is explicit that this is not the original method: §C.1 says they "use an iterative version of the Stochastic Few-Shot method to increase the probability of the target LLM generating a target string". The loss-guided iteration is HarmBench's addition, so 38.3 is a number about HarmBench's variant, not about the 2022 paper. Reach for it when you have weights, want a strong attack, and cannot afford GCG's 500 gradient steps — or when you specifically want to test whether a defense trained against gradients generalises to anything else.

Human — Human Jailbreaks

black box · no model calls to buildclass_name: HumanJailbreaks5 of 114 templates per behaviorTable 6 avg 27.3

The corpus is one Python list, JAILBREAKS, in baselines/human_jailbreaks/jailbreaks.py: 114 entries, about 226,000 characters, median template around 1,400 characters and the longest around 16,000. These are in-the-wild templates of the Do Anything Now lineage — persona framings, fictional-wrapper framings, rule-override framings. This site does not reproduce any of them; the structural facts above are what you can learn from the file without reading it.

Construction is as simple as it looks: template, blank line, behavior string. For contextual behaviors the passage is joined to the behavior first with the same \n\n---\n\n separator DirectRequest uses, and the result is what gets appended. Nothing is adapted per model, per behavior, or per response. The reported column uses experiment_name_template: random_subset_5 with seed: 1 — and note that the list is reshuffled inside the per-behavior loop, so each behavior draws a different five of the 114 while the whole sequence stays deterministic given the seed and the CSV order. Five test cases per behavior; behavior_chunk_size: all_behaviors, base_num_gpus: 0, both target model types allowed.

An all_jailbreaks experiment exists (random_subset: -1), and a HumanJailbreaks-all pipeline row for it is commented out with a warning that it is expensive on closed-source models. It would be: roughly 113 templates × 400 text behaviors is about 45,000 completions at 512 tokens each, before the classifier pass. Roughly 113, not 114, because -1 is an integer, so the subset slice JAILBREAKS[:-1] takes all but the last entry of the shuffled list — a harmless off-by-one that does not touch the reported column, but does mean the "use all jailbreaks" path runs 113 of them.

At 27.3 the whole celebrated corpus is worth 2.0 points over sending the request plainly. By slice: 31.9 standard, 41.4 contextual, 4.3 copyright. The per-model numbers say why, and they say it unusually cleanly. Across two revisions of the same closed model, Human Jailbreaks drops off a cliff: GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 scores 24.5 and GPT-3.5 Turbo 1106 scores 2.8; GPT-4 0613 scores 11.3 and GPT-4 Turbo 1106 scores 2.6. The three Claude models sit at 2.4, 0.3 and 0.3. Public templates, published and indexed, against vendors who have had them for years.

Be careful what you conclude from that. This is five unadapted templates out of a 2023 snapshot, thrown at models trained after the snapshot, scored on the mean of the five rather than on whether any worked. It is a fair measurement of do the famous jailbreaks still work — largely no — and it is not a measurement of can a human jailbreak this model, which is a different question involving a person who adapts after each refusal, and which HarmBench does not ask of any of its 18 methods. Source: Shen et al. 2023, "Do Anything Now": Characterizing and Evaluating In-The-Wild Jailbreak Prompts on Large Language Models (arXiv:2308.03825). Reach for it as a staleness check on your own safety training: a model that still falls to a 2023 template has a problem that required no adaptive attack to find.

ArtPrompt

black box + one GPT-4 API callclass_name: ArtPrompt1 masking call, 1 test case per behaviornot in Table 6

State the gap first: ArtPrompt has no HarmBench ASR number. It appears nowhere in the arXiv v2 text and in none of the extracted result tables — the paper's headline grid has sixteen attack columns and this is not one of them. It is in the repository's method set as a later addition, which is why the repo's 21 method configs and 24 active pipeline entries are a superset of the paper's 18 compared methods. Anything you see quoted as "ArtPrompt's HarmBench score" did not come from the paper.

Mechanism, at the level this site will describe it: a masking model is asked which single word in the request carries the sensitive load; that word is replaced by a placeholder in the text, and the word itself is re-presented in a different channel, rendered as ASCII art. The safety layer reading the prompt as text never sees the word; a model capable of reading the rendering can still recover it. The paper's framing is that alignment training assumes prompts are interpreted semantically, and that assumption has a hole. Going further than this — the rendering scheme, the instruction that accompanies it — would be a build guide, so this page stops here; the repository has the code if you have a reason to read it.

The port's details are informative even so. masking_model: gpt-4-0613 and masking_model_key is required — the constructor raises without it — so despite base_num_gpus: 0 this method needs an OpenAI account to generate test cases at all. masking_direction: horizontal by default with vertical available; fontname: cards from a six-item choice list (gen, alphabet, letters, keyboard, cards, puzzle) — and that list is the intersection of the two renderers, since the horizontal class implements eleven fonts and the vertical only those six. Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 5, both target model types allowed.

Three implementation facts a reader should know before trusting a number from it. Only the first returned mask word is used — the code carries a comment saying multi-word masking is unsupported. Candidate words containing a space are discarded, and if nothing usable survives, the test case falls back to the plain behavior string, which is to say it silently degrades to DirectRequest; a run's real ArtPrompt coverage is therefore a fraction you would have to measure, not assume. And if the masking model returns a word that is not a literal substring of the request, the run raises rather than degrading. There is also vestigial optimiser plumbing: targets_path is required and loaded but never read, and each log entry records loss=0. Nothing here is optimised.

Primary source: Jiang et al. 2024, ArtPrompt: ASCII Art-based Jailbreak Attacks against Aligned LLMs (arXiv:2402.11753) — the paper that also introduces the ViTC (Vision-in-Text Challenge) benchmark the code's class names refer to. Reach for it as a channel test rather than a strength test: it asks whether your safety layer only looks at the semantics of text, which is a different question from whether your model can be argued out of a refusal.

DR — DirectRequest

black box · no attackclass_name: DirectRequestno hyperparameters at allTable 6 avg 25.3

The entire method config is placeholder: placeholder, written twice — once under default_method_hyperparameters and once under the default experiment. There is nothing to tune because there is nothing being done. generate_test_cases returns the Behavior string. Pipeline row: class_name: DirectRequest, experiment_name_template: default, behavior_chunk_size: all_behaviors, base_num_gpus: 0, both model types. Step 1 of the pipeline is string formatting on a CPU; the entire cost of the method lives in generation and classification.

One detail matters for reproduction. For a contextual behavior it does not send the behavior alone: it sends ContextString, then \n\n---\n\n, then the behavior. That exact join is shared by Human Jailbreaks, ZeroShot and SFS, which makes it an undocumented convention of the method set rather than a quirk of one file. A harness that reimplements "just send the behavior" without it is not running DirectRequest and its floor will be wrong — low, and wrong in the direction that flatters every attack measured against it.

"This tests how well models can refuse direct requests to engage in the behaviors when the requests are not obfuscated in any way and often suggest malicious intent."— HarmBench, §C.1

25.3 average. The per-model range is the finding: 0.8 on Llama 2 7B Chat, 65.8 on Zephyr 7B — two models the same benchmark treats as comparable rows, eighty-fold apart before anyone attacks anything. Alongside them sit SOLAR 10.7B-Instruct at 61.3, Starling 7B at 57.0, Mixtral 8x7B at 47.3 and Mistral 7B at 46.3, against 2.8 for both the 13B and 70B Llama 2 Chat models. Refusal training, not scale, is what separates them — the point the results page makes across the whole grid.

By functional slice, 23.9 standard / 46.2 contextual / 7.3 copyright, and two things fall out of that. First, contextual is nearly double standard: paste the passage, ask plainly, and 46% of the contextual behaviors succeed with no attack whatsoever — the sharpest form of the paper's Figure 11 claim that the harder-to-look-up behaviors are the easier ones to elicit. Second, on the Copyright Violations slice DirectRequest's 7.3 is above GCG's 4.6 and above every gradient column in the grid. That is not a fluke: the MinHash grader only fires on near-verbatim regurgitation, and adversarial optimisation pushes a model off verbatim reproduction. Asking politely is a better copyright attack than optimising, and the best method in that slice overall is TAP at 8.5. (These are the recomputed copyright averages; the printed Average row in the v2 copyright sub-table is inconsistent with its own cells — see the discrepancy note.)

It has a multimodal twin, MultiModalDirectRequest, which on the 110 multimodal behaviors scores 69.09 on LLaVA 1.5 and 16.36 on GPT-4V — the same lesson in a second modality. Reach for it always, first, before anything else: it is the control, and it is free.

Read the family top to bottom and the argument is compact. Take an attacker LLM and let it guess (ZeroShot, 25.4) and you have gained nothing over asking. Give that same attacker LLM a target-string objective and fifty rounds of selection on its own best outputs (SFS, 38.3) and you have gained thirteen points and passed three of the white-box gradient methods. The delta between those two rows is the whole value of a feedback loop, measured with the attack model held constant — which is a cleaner ablation than the benchmark advertises.

Reading any ASR number against the floor

Here is the practical instruction this page exists to give. Never report an attack ASR on its own. Report the pair.

Zephyr 7B under GCG is 69.5; Llama 2 7B Chat under GCG is 32.5. Read alone, Zephyr looks twice as broken. Read against the floor, Zephyr went 65.8 → 69.5 — a model that barely needed attacking, where the adversarial work bought 3.7 points — and Llama 2 7B Chat went 0.8 → 32.5, a model whose refusals hold until someone optimises against them and then do not. Those are opposite security stories with adjacent headline numbers, and only the pair distinguishes them. The R2D2 row is the same lesson turned up: 14.2 at the floor, 5.5 under the attack it was trained against, 60.8 under TAP. An ASR is a claim about the attack you ran.

Two more cautions before you use any of these numbers. First, the grader agrees with humans about 93% of the time, so roughly 7% of labels are noise: the 2.0 points between Human Jailbreaks and DirectRequest, and the 0.1 between ZeroShot and DirectRequest, are not differences you can defend. The 13 points between SFS and ZeroShot are. Second, over-refusal is not measured anywhere in HarmBench. DirectRequest is the closest thing the benchmark has to a capability signal — and it is not one, because a model that refuses every benign request scores 0 here and looks flawless. Pair it with something that measures false refusals or you have optimised for the wrong thing; the classifier page lists the rest of the gaps.

What makes this family the one that keeps an evaluation honest is that each member isolates exactly one variable, and none of them has a compute story to hide behind. DirectRequest isolates nothing — it is the control. ZeroShot isolates rephrasing. SFS isolates target-guided search with the attack model held fixed. Human Jailbreaks isolates the public prior: everything an adversary could have found by reading the internet in 2023. ArtPrompt isolates the input channel. Run all five before you run anything expensive, and you will usually find that the interesting question about your model was answered by the cheapest one. The behavior set they all draw from is the same 510 rows, browsable at the behavior index.
