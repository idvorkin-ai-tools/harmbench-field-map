Gradient and token-level attacks

The family that sets the ceiling in the results table, eats the GPU budget, and leaves the closed-model rows blank.
TL;DR — Seven methods, one idea: append a twenty-token suffix to the request and search token space for the string that makes an affirmative continuation most likely. That search needs a backward pass, so it needs weights — which is why six of these seven columns are dashes for every API-only model in Table 6. GCG's greedy coordinate search is the strongest attack in the paper at 54.3 average ASR and much the most expensive: 500 steps × 512 candidate forward passes per behavior per model. The three continuous-relaxation methods reach 29.0–30.8 for a fraction of that, which says discrete search over real tokens is doing most of the work. GCG-Transfer is the escape hatch: build one suffix against four open models, reuse it anywhere — the only reason this family has a GPT-4 number.

This family decides how the whole benchmark reads. It contains the strongest attack, so it fixes the top of every model's row; it consumes almost all of the compute, so it fixes what a full run costs; and it is structurally unable to attack the models most readers care about, so it fixes the shape of the results grid — dense numbers on the open-weight models, a wall of dashes on GPT-4, Claude and Gemini. Understanding why those dashes are there is most of what you need to read the table honestly.

One objective, seven search strategies

Every method here builds a test case the same way: take the behavior's request exactly as the CSV ships it, append a suffix of twenty tokens, and let the suffix be the only thing that varies. For a contextual behavior the context passage is prepended ahead of the request, so the optimised tokens still sit at the end of the user turn and the chat template wraps around them untouched. The suffix is initialised to twenty repeated exclamation marks — a deliberately meaningless placeholder that carries no information into the search.

The objective is identical too. Each behavior has a fixed target string: a short affirmative opening that a compliant model would produce if it were about to carry the behavior out. The loss is the mean token-level cross-entropy of that target given everything before it, so minimising it makes the affirmative opening the model's most likely continuation. Those target strings sit in one JSON file in the repository, and it is the one artefact this map deliberately does not open — nothing here needs it, and it is the closest thing in the repo to a ready-made payload.

Two consequences matter. First, the loss knows nothing about harm. It measures whether the model started a sentence a particular way; the Llama 2 classifier decides afterwards, from a 512-token generation, whether the behavior was exhibited. The two judgements disagree often enough that GCG's own implementation stops trusting the loss: every 50 steps, once the loss falls below 0.05, it generates a completion, checks it against a refusal-prefix heuristic, and exits only if the generation is not a refusal.

Second, the gradient requires the weights. It is taken with respect to a one-hot encoding of the suffix tokens, which means a backward pass through the target plus direct access to its embedding matrix. No API exposes that, and the runner enforces it rather than leaving it to the user: each entry in run_pipeline.yaml carries an allowed_target_model_types list, and any target whose model_type in models.yaml is missing from it is silently skipped when experiments are expanded. Six of these seven list open_source only. That config field, not any property of the models, is what produces the dashes.

One more framing fact, and it is why this family is comparable at all rather than a bag of unrelated papers:

"Methods labeled with ∗ were adapted using insights from GCG, following the evaluation in Zou et al. (2023)."— HarmBench, §C.1

PEZ, GBDA, UAT and AutoPrompt all carry that asterisk. None was proposed as a jailbreak method — they are a prompt-discovery technique, a text-adversarial-example technique, an NLP-analysis technique and a knowledge-elicitation technique, retrofitted here with GCG's suffix length, initialisation and affirmative-target loss. The seven numbers below are therefore close to a controlled experiment: same objective, same search space, different strategy for choosing what to try next.

The seven methods

GCG — Greedy Coordinate Gradient

white box · weights + embeddingsclass GCG500 steps × 512 candidatesavg ASR 54.3

One step has four parts. (1) A single backward pass gives the gradient of the loss with respect to the suffix's one-hot matrix — a grid of (20 positions × vocabulary), each entry a first-order estimate of what swapping that token into that slot would do to the loss. (2) The top 256 tokens per position are kept, and 512 candidate suffixes are assembled, each differing from the current one in exactly one slot; the slots are spread evenly over all twenty positions and the replacement is drawn uniformly from that position's top-256. (3) Candidates that do not survive a decode-then-re-encode round trip are dropped — a suffix that re-tokenises differently is not the string the optimiser thinks it is — shrinking the effective search width. (4) Every survivor is scored with a real forward pass; the argmin becomes the new suffix.

The gradient only proposes; the forward passes decide. That is the whole design, and it is why GCG costs what it does: 500 steps of one backward pass plus ~512 forward passes each, on the order of a quarter of a million candidate evaluations for one behavior on one model. Three details keep it from being worse. use_prefix_cache: True caches the KV states of everything before the suffix, so each candidate only re-runs the suffix and target tokens — added specifically because contextual behaviors carry long prefixes, and disabled per-model for Baichuan 2 and Qwen, which mishandle a cache during optimisation. allow_non_ascii: False restricts candidates to printable-ASCII tokens. And the candidate batch size auto-halves on OOM instead of failing the job.

Config: num_steps: 500, search_width: 512, num_test_cases_per_behavior: 1, eval_steps: 50 with eval_with_check_refusal: True and check_refusal_min_loss: 0.05; early_stopping: False, so the refusal check is the only early exit. Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 1 — one job per behavior, 400 jobs per target for the text set (full ID list: all 510 behaviors) — base_num_gpus: 0, allowed_target_model_types: [open_source].

Primary paper: Zou, Wang, Carlini, Nasr, Kolter & Fredrikson, Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models, arXiv:2307.15043 (2023). Reach for GCG when you have weights, GPU-days, and want the strongest answer to "can this model be made to do this at all" — an upper bound on the family, not a realistic threat model.

GCG-Multi — the multi-behavior suffix

white box · one target modelclass EnsembleGCG500 steps, all behaviors per job, 5 runsavg ASR 45.0

Same step structure, but one suffix is optimised for many behaviors at once against a single model. Gradients are computed per behavior, averaged and L2-normalised per position; candidates are scored on every active behavior and the winner has the lowest mean loss. Behaviors enter on a curriculum: the run starts with one and admits the next either when every active behavior's loss under the chosen candidate drops below 0.2, or after 100 steps without an admission.

That curriculum has a consequence the config does not advertise. With behavior_chunk_size: all_behaviors one job owns the entire behavior set, but 500 steps admit only as many behaviors as the loss allows — and when the run ends, every behavior in the chunk is issued the final shared suffix as its test case, including the ones the curriculum never reached. That is the intended semantics, since this is a test of universality rather than per-behavior fit, and it is much of why GCG-Multi averages 45.0 against GCG's 54.3. The implementation runs one Ray actor per model and asserts all tokenizers are the same type.

Config: EnsembleGCG defaults — num_steps: 500, search_width: 512, eval_steps: 10, progressive_behaviors on. Pipeline row: template <model_name>, behavior_chunk_size: all_behaviors, base_num_gpus: 0, run_ids: [0,1,2,3,4] — five independent runs merged into five test cases per behavior, so the ASR is an average over five suffixes rather than one lucky one — allowed_target_model_types: [open_source]. Primary paper: same as GCG, arXiv:2307.15043. Reach for it when the question is "does one string break this model generally", not "can this model be broken on this request".

GCG-Transfer — the universal suffix

white box to build, no access to useclass EnsembleGCG1000 steps × 4 attack models, 5 runsavg ASR 38.8

The same EnsembleGCG code, but the gradient and the candidate loss are averaged across four attack models rather than one: Llama 2 7B Chat, Vicuna 7B v1.3, Llama 2 13B Chat and Vicuna 13B v1.3, one Ray worker each, at 1000 steps instead of 500. The pipeline entry is a fixed experiment name rather than a per-model template — llama2_7b_vicuna_7b_llama2_13b_vicuna_13b_multibehavior_1000steps — because the artefact it produces has nothing to do with the model it will eventually be pointed at. The Vicuna pin to v1.3 is deliberate, with a config comment saying it follows the original GCG paper's transfer experiments.

This is the only entry in the family whose allowed_target_model_types includes closed_source, and the reason is economic rather than technical. The expensive part happened once, offline, on models whose weights were available; using the result against a new target costs exactly one API call per behavior. Every gradient-family number you will ever see for an API model comes from here: GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 38.9 and 1106 42.5, GPT-4 0613 22.0, GPT-4 Turbo 1106 22.3, Claude 1 12.1, Claude 2 2.7, Claude 2.1 2.6, Gemini Pro 18.0. It is also the cheapest way to get any number for a closed model, which is why it is worth understanding what that number means: it measures how well a suffix built against Llama 2 and Vicuna happens to carry over, and it is a floor on the target's vulnerability rather than an estimate of its robustness.

Transfer is not uniformly worse than attacking the target directly. On Orca 2 7B, GCG-Transfer scores 60.1 against GCG-Multi's 38.7 and even beats per-behavior GCG's 46.0 — a suffix tuned on four other models does better on that target than the search run against the target itself. With about 7% label noise in the grader, a 14-point gap like that is real.

Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: all_behaviors, base_num_gpus: 4, run_ids: [0,1,2,3,4], allowed_target_model_types: [open_source, closed_source]. Primary paper: arXiv:2307.15043. Reach for it when you have no parameter access and want a white-box-derived number anyway — or when you want to know whether a defended model is robust to strings it has never seen.

AutoPrompt (AP) — GCG's ancestor

white box · weights + embeddingsclass AutoPrompt500 steps × 512 candidatesavg ASR 43.7

The config file is nearly a copy of GCG's — 500 steps, search width 512, the same twenty-token initialisation, the same ASCII restriction, the same prefix cache, the same refusal-check exit — and the implementation differs in exactly one function. GCG builds its 512 candidates by spreading substitutions across all twenty slots; AutoPrompt picks one position at random per step and puts all 512 candidates in that slot. The code comments the difference explicitly. Same gradient, same budget, same forward-pass count: the only variable is which candidate set gets scored.

The 10.6-point average gap (43.7 against 54.3) is therefore a clean measurement of what coordinate-wise coverage buys, and the gap is very unevenly distributed. On weakly-aligned targets it barely matters — Vicuna 7B 56.3 against GCG's 65.5, Mistral 7B 62.7 against 69.8. On the hard targets it is decisive: Llama 2 7B Chat 15.3 against 32.5, Llama 2 13B Chat 16.3 against 30.0. Concentrating a step's budget on one slot is fine when almost any suffix works and expensive when the model actually resists.

Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 1, base_num_gpus: 0, allowed_target_model_types: [open_source]. Primary paper: Shin, Razeghi, Logan IV, Wallace & Singh, AutoPrompt: Eliciting Knowledge from Language Models with Automatically Generated Prompts, arXiv:2010.15980 (2020) — note the date: this predates jailbreaking as a research topic, and was built to probe what models know. Reach for it only as an ablation of GCG; it costs the same and scores less.

PEZ — projected soft prompts

white box · weights + embeddingsclass PEZ500 Adam steps, 5 test cases in parallelavg ASR 29.0

PEZ abandons the candidate set entirely. It holds twenty continuous embedding vectors as Adam parameters, initialised from randomly chosen vocabulary rows. On each step the forward pass projects every soft vector to its nearest real token embedding by cosine similarity and computes the loss on that projected sequence; the backward pass treats the projection as the identity, a straight-through estimator, so the gradient lands on the continuous parameters. The test case emitted at each step is the projection — always a real token sequence, never a soft prompt the model could not actually be sent.

The cost profile is the opposite of GCG's: one forward and one backward per step instead of one backward plus 512 forwards, for a batch of five test cases at once. At equal step counts that is roughly two orders of magnitude less compute per behavior. What it buys is 29.0 against 54.3. The mechanism explains why: gradient descent in embedding space optimises a point that is only meaningful after rounding, and the loss at the rounded point is not the loss the optimiser was descending. Discrete search never has that problem, because everything it scores is a string the model will actually receive.

Config: num_steps: 500, lr: 0.01 with cosine annealing, num_optim_tokens: 20, num_test_cases_per_behavior: 5, test_cases_batch_size: 5 (both dropped to 1 for Llama 2 70B and Qwen 72B, with a config comment that one is sufficient and the big models are expensive). Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 5, base_num_gpus: 0, allowed_target_model_types: [open_source].

Primary paper: Wen, Jain, Kirchenbauer, Goldblum, Geiping & Goldstein, Hard Prompts Made Easy: Gradient-Based Discrete Optimization for Prompt Tuning and Discovery, arXiv:2302.03668 (2023) — a prompt-discovery method, not an attack. Reach for it when compute, not success rate, is the binding constraint.

GBDA — Gumbel-softmax relaxation

white box · weights + embeddingsclass GBDA500 Adam steps, 5 test cases in parallelavg ASR 29.8

Where PEZ relaxes the embedding, GBDA relaxes the choice. The optimised object is a matrix of logits over the vocabulary, one distribution per suffix position, initialised at zero plus Gaussian noise of scale 0.2. Each step draws a soft Gumbel-softmax sample from those logits, mixes the embedding matrix by the resulting weights to get twenty blended vectors, forwards them, and backpropagates into the logits with Adam. The softmax temperature is annealed linearly from 1.0 down to 0.1 across the run, so early steps optimise a genuinely smooth blend of tokens and late steps optimise something close to a hard selection. The emitted test case is the per-position argmax.

Two things are worth flagging. The blended input the optimiser sees is not a sequence the model could ever be sent — it is a convex combination of token embeddings — so the same rounding gap that limits PEZ applies here, softened but not removed by the temperature schedule. And HarmBench's implementation carries only the target cross-entropy: the fluency and semantic-similarity regularisers from the original paper are not in the loss. That is consistent with the asterisk in §C.1 — the method was adapted to GCG's setup — but it means what is measured here is the relaxation, not the published attack.

Config: num_optim_tokens: 20, num_steps: 500, lr: 0.2, noise_scale: 0.2, num_test_cases_per_behavior: 5, test_cases_batch_size: 5. Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 5, base_num_gpus: 0, allowed_target_model_types: [open_source].

Primary paper: Guo, Sablayrolles, Jégou & Kiela, Gradient-based Adversarial Attacks against Text Transformers, arXiv:2104.13733 (2021). Reach for it in the same situations as PEZ; the two are within a point of each other on average and within the grader's noise on most individual models.

UAT — Universal Adversarial Triggers

white box · weights + embeddingsclass UAT≤100 sweeps × 20 positions × 3 candidatesavg ASR 30.8

UAT is the cheapest discrete method here and the clearest illustration of what search width buys. Each outer step walks the twenty positions in order. At a position, a backward pass through a hook on the embedding layer gives the gradient at that slot; the HotFlip rule — a first-order Taylor estimate of the loss change from a swap, computed as the dot product of that gradient with every row of the embedding matrix — ranks the whole vocabulary, and only the top three candidates are actually forwarded and scored. The best is kept if it improves the running loss. When a full sweep of all twenty positions produces no improvement, the run stops.

At most 100 sweeps × 20 positions × 3 candidates is on the order of six thousand candidate evaluations per behavior, against GCG's quarter-million — roughly forty times cheaper for 30.8 against 54.3. It confirms the family's central trade directly: the gradient is a good enough proposal distribution to make cheap search work at all, and nowhere near good enough to replace the scoring pass.

One caveat on the name. The original method optimises a single trigger over a whole dataset; HarmBench runs it per behavior, as a single-behavior method with num_test_cases_per_behavior: 1. The universality that the paper's title advertises is not what the 30.8 is measuring — GCG-Multi and GCG-Transfer are this family's actual universal attacks.

Config: num_steps: 100, num_candidates: 3, eval_steps: 2, use_prefix_cache: True, same twenty-token initialisation. Pipeline row: behavior_chunk_size: 5, base_num_gpus: 0, allowed_target_model_types: [open_source]. Primary paper: Wallace, Feng, Kandpal, Gardner & Singh, Universal Adversarial Triggers for Attacking and Analyzing NLP, arXiv:1908.07125 (2019).

What these numbers say

The strongest attack is also the one nobody runs at scale. The paper's own aside, written while explaining why adversarial training against GCG was hard to build, is the most useful cost figure in the whole document:

"GCG is extremely slow, requiring ~20 minutes to generate a single test case on 7B parameter LLMs using an A100."— HarmBench, §5

Multiply that out. The text set is 400 behaviors, one job each, so a single 7B target costs on the order of 130 A100-hours for the GCG column alone, and the grid carries GCG numbers for roughly twenty open-weight targets at sizes up to 70B. That is where the benchmark's compute goes, and it is why the precomputed results archive on Zenodo is 10.4 GB of other people's GPU time rather than something you casually regenerate. If you are budgeting a HarmBench run, this family is the decision; everything on the LLM-optimizer and template pages costs a rounding error by comparison.

The 54 versus 29 gap is a statement about hard targets, not about attacks in general. Averages hide this completely. On Zephyr 7B, PEZ scores 62.5 and GCG 69.5 — seven points apart, on a model that barely resists anything. On Llama 2 7B Chat, PEZ scores 1.8 and GCG 32.5, a factor of eighteen. GBDA and UAT behave the same way. So the relaxation methods are not uniformly weaker; they are adequate against models with little refusal training and near-useless against models with a lot of it, which is precisely the regime anyone actually cares about. Read a relaxation-method number on a robust model as evidence about the method, not about the model.

Two open-weight models still carry dashes. Qwen 72B Chat and Mixtral 8x7B have no GCG, GCG-Multi, AutoPrompt, PEZ, GBDA or UAT numbers — only GCG-Transfer, at 36.2 and 62.5. Their weights are public and their configs exist in the repo, so this is not an access boundary; the paper does not say why, and the plain reading is that per-behavior white-box optimisation at that size did not fit the compute budget. Cost, not capability, is what redacted those cells — a useful reminder when comparing a 7B row against a 72B one.

Copyright is a floor for this family, for a reason that has nothing to do with the search. Against copyright behaviors, GCG averages about 4.6 — near the bottom of its range — because those behaviors are graded by MinHash against shipped reference hashes, not by the classifier, and the metric only fires on near-verbatim regurgitation. Optimising an affirmative opening does not make a model reproduce a text it will not reproduce. Note also that the arXiv v2 copyright sub-table prints an Average row inconsistent with its own cells; the reconciliation is on the results page.

And this family is exactly the one a defense can overfit to. Zephyr 7B + R2D2 — adversarially trained against a refreshed pool of GCG test cases — goes 69.5 → 5.5 under GCG, 0.0 under GCG-Transfer, 0.0 under UAT, 0.2 under GBDA. Read only this page's columns and you would call it solved. Its PAIR number is 48.0 and its TAP number is 60.8. Training against a gradient adversary bought robustness to gradient adversaries; the defenses page works through what that implies. Two closing cautions for anyone reporting numbers from here: quote DirectRequest (25.3 average) alongside them, because a gradient attack that beats an unmodified request by five points has demonstrated very little; and remember the grader agrees with humans about 93% of the time, so GBDA at 29.8 and PEZ at 29.0 are not a ranking.
