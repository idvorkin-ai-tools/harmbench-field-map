Defenses: R2D2 and the read-across caveat

The paper's own defense, the capability bill it comes with, and the one lesson from this map worth carrying into every other benchmark you read.

The three pages before this built the number: the grader, the pipeline, the grid. This one is about trying to move it down. HarmBench's authors built a defense with their own benchmark in the loop and printed its full row — the most instructive object in the paper, not because the defense is weak, but because it is genuinely strong in one place and visibly weak in another, and both facts sit in the same line of the same table.

R2D2: adversarial training against an adversary that keeps moving

Almost every defended model in the wild is defended the same way: assemble a static set of harmful prompts, fine-tune the model to refuse them, ship. The problem is that the attacker optimises after you stop, and GCG searches token by token against your specific weights. The textbook answer is to put the attacker inside the training loop, and for LLMs it is unaffordable: GCG needs about twenty minutes per test case against a 7B model on an A100, so a loop regenerating attacks every step spends all its time attacking and none learning. Earlier attempts froze the adversary — never re-optimising against the current weights — and reported disappointing results.

Robust Refusal Dynamic Defense (R2D2) borrows the trick that made adversarial training affordable in vision: persistent adversarial examples. Rather than restarting the attack each step, keep a standing pool of 180 test cases. Each iteration samples 8, runs GCG for only 5 further steps to bring them up to date against the weights as they now are, computes the losses on those refreshed cases, and takes a model step. The attack is never solved from scratch and never fully stale — it tracks the model. To stop the pool ossifying, 20% of it is randomly reset every 50 model updates. Total: 500 steps, roughly 16 hours on an 8×A100 node. A node-day, not a research programme.

The base model is Mistral 7B, the instruction data is UltraChat, and the harness is the Zephyr 7B Beta SFT training code with the R2D2 machinery spliced into the trainer. That last detail matters, and we come back to it.

The two losses, and why one of them is not obvious

Each step minimises three terms added together. The SFT loss is ordinary instruction tuning on UltraChat, present only to stop the model forgetting how to be useful. The toward loss is the one anybody would write — −log fθ(trefusal | xi), meaning "given this adversarial prompt, make a refusal likely". In the shipped trainer that refusal target is one hard-coded sentence, identical for every test case. The third term is the interesting one:

L_away = -1 · log( 1 - f_θ(t_i | x_i) )

where ti is the harmful continuation GCG was optimising toward and xi is the adversarial prompt. It is the exact negation of the GCG objective: whatever GCG just spent five steps making probable, this pushes back down.

Why is that not redundant with the toward loss? Because "make refusal likely" and "make this specific completion unlikely" are different objectives, and a model can satisfy the first while failing the second. Output is a distribution over an enormous space; raising one refusal string from 0.2 to 0.6 says almost nothing about a particular harmful continuation sitting at 0.05, because the mass may have come from a thousand other sequences. And 0.05 is not safe — the adversary keeps searching, and greedy decoding only needs the harmful branch to win token by token where it matters. The toward loss shapes the mode; the away loss attacks the specific tail the attacker is aiming at. Train only the first and you get a model that refuses confidently right up until a suffix flips it.

The implementation adds a detail the formula does not show: the away term is computed per token with a floor, so once a token's log P falls below a threshold (−5.0 in the shipped code, roughly 0.7% probability) its contribution is zeroed. Otherwise −log(1−p) keeps returning gradient on tokens already effectively impossible, and the away term — unbounded in the useful direction — swamps the SFT term and eats the model's general ability.

The shipped defaults and the reported run do not fully agree. The training script matches the paper on the per-step knobs — 5 GCG steps per iteration, 8 cases refreshed per step, a reset every 50 steps, search width 512 — but ships a pool of 8 test cases rather than 180, and the repo README calls the released checkpoint (cais/zephyr_7b_r2d2) "a snapshot after 2000 steps" against the paper's M = 500. The multi-behavior path is commented out and marked unsupported.

The headline deserves its headline. 5.5 under GCG is the best result any model-level defense in the paper achieves against the strongest attack in the paper, beating Llama 2 13B Chat's 30.0 and Llama 2 70B Chat's 37.5 — models whose refusal behaviour is the output of a full industrial RLHF programme. They pull both rows down about equally, so the comparison is fair, but these are not uniform "how often did it comply" figures.

Read it honestly. R2D2 lands at 6.0 — 1.34 MT-Bench points below its own Zephyr base, below Mistral 7B's 6.5, above Koala 13B's 5.4. The paper frames this as "does not necessarily harm performance", fair as "still a usable assistant" and generous as a reading of a 1.34-point drop on a ten-point scale. The robustness gain is enormous and the capability cost is real and not zero. Trading 43.6 points of average ASR for 1.34 of MT-Bench is an excellent deal, and it is still a trade.

The comparison also carries an asymmetry the paper states plainly. The Zephyr 7B in the main tables is Zephyr 7B Beta, SFT and DPO. R2D2 is built on the SFT-only variant, with no DPO stage. So the 7.34 → 6.0 gap is not purely the price of adversarial training — part of it is the missing preference-optimisation stage. The same asymmetry sits under the 69.5 → 5.5 robustness claim in the other direction: the two rows do not differ only by R2D2. This is a preliminary demonstration, not a controlled ablation, and the paper says so — worth repeating precisely because so few defense papers state theirs.

Read the row across, not down

Look at the sorted table again and ignore the absolute numbers. The top — UAT, GCG-Transfer, GBDA, PEZ, GCG-Multi, GCG, AutoPrompt — is the entire gradient family, every method that searches token sequences using the model's own gradients. All seven collapse, five below 5%. The bottom — TAP, PAIR, TAP-Transfer, PAP-top5, Stochastic Few-Shot — is almost exactly the LLM-driven family, where another model writes and iteratively refines a natural-language jailbreak from what the target did last time. Those barely move: a 12.6× reduction in one column and 1.1× in another, same model, same behaviors, same training run.

The middle shows the split is not simply gradient versus not. DirectRequest, Human Jailbreaks and ZeroShot also fall a long way (4.6×, 4.9×, 8.3×) with no gradients involved. What they share is being non-adaptive — a fixed request, a fixed template, a prompt written blind. Plain refusal training handles those, and the toward loss gives R2D2 that for free. What survives is the intersection: attacks that adapt to this model in a search space R2D2 never trained against. GCG adapts in token space, where the defense lives; PAIR and TAP adapt in semantic space, where it has nothing to say.

Generalise it, because this is the lesson the whole map turns on. An ASR number is a claim about the attack you ran, not about the model. "Zephyr + R2D2 has 5.5% ASR" and "Zephyr + R2D2 has 60.8% ASR" are both true of the same weights, on the same 510 behaviors, under the same classifier, differing only in which attack produced the test cases. Anyone reporting a single robustness figure without naming the attacks behind it is reporting a coincidence — specifically, a coincidence about which attacks the authors chose to run, which correlates strongly with which attacks they were defending against. That is the practical content of the paper's broader finding that no model is robust to all attacks and no attack breaks all models. A robustness claim needs an attack list attached or it means nothing.

Credit where due: the paper says this about its own method, unprompted, in the section announcing the method's success.

"For some attacks, the improvement conferred by R2D2 is less pronounced. This is especially true for methods dissimilar to the train-time GCG adversary, including PAIR, TAP, and Stochastic Few-Shot. This suggests that incorporating multiple diverse attacks into adversarial training may be necessary to obtain generalizable robustness."— HarmBench, §6, Adversarial Training Results

The normal shape of a defense paper is a table of the attacks the defense handles plus a discussion of why the others were out of scope. Here the weak columns are printed at the same width as the strong ones in the main results table — only possible because the authors fixed the battery before building the defense. That ordering is the real argument for benchmarks like this one.

One caution on the deltas: the grader agrees with humans about 93% of the time, so roughly 7% label noise sits under every cell. Nowhere near enough to threaten 69.5 → 5.5; entirely enough to make 66.5 versus 60.8 a difference you should not lean on. The read-across argument holds because the extremes differ by an order of magnitude, not because any small movement is meaningful.

The other "defenses" among the 33 targets

"33 target LLMs and defenses" invites a misreading. R2D2 is the only defense method HarmBench implements. Everything else in that count is a shipped model whose vendor safety-trained it — a real category, since safety training is a model-level defense, but there is no defense code, no config, nothing to reproduce. Three groups from Table 6:

  • The Llama 2 Chat family — what good refusal training looks like. 32.5 / 30.0 / 37.5 under GCG for 7B / 13B / 70B, against 0.8 / 2.8 / 2.8 under DirectRequest. That shape is the signature of serious RLHF refusal training: nothing gets through if you merely ask, and a white-box optimizer holding the weights still gets through about a third of the time. Note 70B is less robust than 13B — scale does not buy robustness, the pipeline does.
  • The Claude models — the lowest numbers in the entire table. Claude 2 and 2.1 at 2.7 and 2.6 under GCG-Transfer, under 5 on every column they were run against, 0.3 under Human Jailbreaks. Claude 1 is higher at 12.1. Being API-only, they have no gradient columns at all.
  • The GPT models — safety-trained and clearly penetrable by transfer. GPT-4 0613 and GPT-4 Turbo 1106 hold DirectRequest to 21.0 and 9.3 and GCG-Transfer to 22.0 and 22.3, yet both exceed 54 under TAP-Transfer; GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 reaches 62.3 there. Transfer attacks are what populate the closed-model columns at all, and what these models are worst against.
Read Claude's row twice. A near-zero row is consistent with two very different models: one that refuses harmful requests while staying helpful on everything else, and one that refuses too much. HarmBench cannot tell them apart — no benign set ships, no false-refusal measurement, and no capability check other than the single MT-Bench score the authors ran on their own defended model. A model that refuses everything scores 0% ASR and tops the table. Whatever Claude's numbers mean, they do not by themselves mean "well-calibrated"; that is a claim this benchmark is structurally unable to make. See the classifier's failure modes.

Which is why the MT-Bench line does more work than it looks like it does: it is the only evidence in the paper that any defended model on the list is still useful. Without it, "average ASR 19.1" and "refuses everything" would be indistinguishable from the grid.

Why system-level defenses are out of scope

Defenses split in two. Model-level defenses change the model: safety training, refusal mechanisms, system prompts, adversarial training. System-level defenses wrap it: input filters, output classifiers, prompt cleansing, perplexity checks, a second model screening the request. HarmBench evaluates the first and deliberately excludes the second, and §B.3 gives the reason:

"[W]hen assessing the robustness of a defense, it is vital to consider adaptive attacks, yet adaptive attacks for system-level defenses are highly specific to the individual defense. This makes it challenging to determine whether a defense is truly robust or simply hasn't been adequately tested (Athalye et al., 2018)."— HarmBench, §B.3

The citation compresses the whole argument. Athalye, Carlini and Wagner (2018) took nine defenses from a single ICLR and broke seven; the common failure was not that the defenses were wrong in principle but that each made gradients useless to the standard attack in a way the standard attack had not been written to handle. Run the fixed battery, get a beautiful number, publish; write the attack that knows about the defense and the number evaporates. The general form: a defense evaluated only against attacks that predate it is untested, and its score is unfalsifiable rather than good.

Apply that to a filter. An input filter rejecting high-perplexity text stops GCG dead, because a GCG suffix is by construction gibberish — and HarmBench's battery would score it near-perfect. The adaptive attack is obvious once you name the defense: add a fluency penalty to the search, or switch to AutoDAN, which already produces readable prompts. But that attack must be written for that filter, and an output classifier needs a different one again. No fixed battery honestly evaluates an arbitrary system-level defense, because the honest evaluation is defense-specific by construction — the generic battery measures the battery's ignorance, not the defense's strength.

Model-level defenses do not have this property to nearly the same degree: the defense is in the weights, the battery's attacks already take the weights (or the API) as input, and there is no separate component for them to be naive about. So a fixed battery gives a meaningful if incomplete reading — and R2D2's row proves it is incomplete in a legible way, because the battery caught the generalisation gap and printed it. HarmBench supports evaluating system-level defenses; it just does not do so itself. Running the battery against your filter is a lower bound on how bad things are, not evidence that things are good.

What to carry away

Always ask which attacks — one family is not a list. Adversarial training generalises less than you hope and further than you might fear: R2D2 transferred cleanly across the whole gradient family, including attacks it never saw, and never crossed into a different search space. And publish the capability number: robustness is trivially achievable by refusing everything, and one line of Table 11 is all that separates R2D2 from a model that scored well by being useless.
