Defenses: R2D2 and the read-across caveat

The paper's own defense, the capability bill it comes with, and the one lesson from this map worth carrying into every other benchmark you read.
TL;DR — HarmBench ships a defense as well as an evaluation: R2D2, adversarial training against a pool of GCG test cases that is re-optimised against the model as the model changes. It works spectacularly in one direction — Zephyr 7B's GCG ASR falls 69.5 → 5.5, past Llama 2 13B Chat's 30.0 — and it costs 1.34 points of MT-Bench. But read the same model's row sideways: 48.0 under PAIR, 54.3 under TAP-Transfer, 60.8 under TAP. Training against a gradient adversary bought robustness to gradient adversaries. The three pages before this one built the number: the grader, the pipeline that produces it, and the grid it fills in. This page is about what happens when you try to move the number down, and it is the page where the map's argument closes. HarmBench's authors did not just measure eighteen attacks against thirty-three targets; they built a defense with the benchmark in the loop and reported its full row. That row is the most instructive object in the paper — not because the defense is weak, but because it is genuinely strong in one place and visibly weak in another, and the two facts sit in the same line of a table where anyone can see them.

R2D2: adversarial training against an adversary that keeps moving

Almost every "defended" model in the wild is defended the same way: someone assembled a static set of harmful prompts, fine-tuned the model to refuse them, and shipped it. The obvious problem is that the attacker gets to optimise after you stop. A gradient attack like GCG searches token by token for a suffix that maximises the probability of a harmful continuation on your specific weights. Refusal training on a fixed prompt list does not anticipate the suffix that GCG will find against the model you shipped.

The textbook answer is adversarial training: put the attacker inside the training loop. For LLMs the textbook answer is unaffordable. GCG takes about twenty minutes to produce one test case against a 7B model on an A100. A training loop that regenerates a fresh batch of attacks at every step would spend essentially all of its time attacking and none of it learning. Prior attempts sidestepped this by freezing the adversary — fine-tuning against a static set of harmful prompts, with no optimisation against the current weights — and reported disappointing results.

Robust Refusal Dynamic Defense (R2D2) borrows the trick that made adversarial training affordable in vision: persistent adversarial examples. Instead of restarting the attack every step, keep a standing pool of 180 test cases. Each training iteration samples 8 of them, runs GCG for only 5 more steps to bring them back up to date against the weights as they now are, computes the losses on those refreshed cases, and takes a model step. The attack is never solved from scratch and never fully stale — it tracks the model. To stop the pool ossifying around a handful of behaviors the optimizer happens to like, 20% of it is randomly reset every 50 model updates. Total: 500 steps, about 16 hours on an 8×A100 node — a single node-day, not a research programme.

The base is Mistral 7B, the instruction data is UltraChat, and the harness is the Zephyr 7B Beta SFT training code with the R2D2 machinery spliced into the trainer. That last detail matters and we return to it below.

The two losses, and why one of them is not obvious

Each step minimises three terms added together. The SFT loss is ordinary instruction tuning on UltraChat and exists only to stop the model forgetting how to be useful. The toward loss is the one everybody would write: −log fθ(trefusal | xi) — given the adversarial prompt, make a fixed refusal sentence likely. In the shipped trainer that refusal target is a single hard-coded string, the same one for every test case.

The third term is the interesting one. The away loss is

L_away = -1 · log( 1 - f_θ(t_i | x_i) )

where ti is the harmful continuation GCG was optimising toward and xi is the adversarial prompt. It is the exact negation of the GCG objective: whatever GCG just spent five steps making probable, this term pushes back down.

Why is that not redundant with the toward loss? Because "make refusal likely" and "make this specific completion unlikely" are different objectives, and a model can satisfy the first while failing the second. Language-model output is a distribution over an enormous space. Raising the probability of one refusal string from 0.2 to 0.6 says almost nothing about what happened to a particular harmful continuation sitting at 0.05 — it may have moved to 0.03, or it may not have moved at all, since the probability mass came out of a thousand other sequences. And 0.05 is not safe: the adversary gets to keep searching, and greedy decoding only needs the harmful branch to win token by token at the point where it matters. The toward loss shapes the mode; the away loss attacks the specific tail the attacker is actually aiming at. Training only the first is how you get a model that is confidently refusing right up until a suffix flips it.

The implementation carries a detail the paper's formula does not show. The away term is computed per token with a floor: once a token's log P under the model has fallen below a threshold (−5.0 in the shipped code, so a probability under about 0.7%), its contribution is zeroed. Without that, −log(1−p) keeps handing back gradient on tokens that are already effectively impossible, and the away term — which is unbounded below in the useful direction — would dominate the SFT term and eat the model's general ability. The threshold is where "push this down" stops and "leave it alone" begins. It is a small line of code doing the load-bearing work of keeping the three losses commensurable.

The repository's shipped defaults and the paper's reported run do not fully agree, and it is worth knowing which is which. The training script's constants match the paper on the per-step knobs — 5 GCG steps per iteration, 8 test cases refreshed per step, a reset every 50 steps, GCG search width 512 — but ship a pool of 8 test cases rather than the paper's 180, and the repo's own README describes the released checkpoint (cais/zephyr_7b_r2d2) as "a snapshot after 2000 steps" while the paper reports M = 500. The multi-behavior test-case path is commented out and marked unsupported. Reproduce the paper's numbers from the shipped defaults and you are running a smaller, longer experiment than the one described.

The headline is real and deserves its headline. 5.5 under GCG is the best result any model-level defense in the paper achieves against the strongest attack in the paper, and it beats Llama 2 13B Chat at 30.0 and Llama 2 70B Chat at 37.5 — models whose refusal behaviour is the product of a full industrial RLHF programme. A single node-day of adversarial training beat that, which is a genuinely surprising result and the reason the method is in the paper at all.

One caveat on the 510-behavior denominator: it includes the 100 copyright behaviors, which are graded by MinHash against shipped hashes and sit near the floor for every model and every attack. They drag both of these rows down by roughly the same amount, so the comparison is fair, but the absolute numbers are not "how often did the model comply" on a uniform test — see the copyright category page for why that column behaves the way it does.

What it cost: Table 11

The paper is one of few in this literature to publish a capability number alongside a robustness number. Table 11:

Read it honestly. R2D2 lands at 6.0 — 1.34 MT-Bench points below its own Zephyr base, below Mistral 7B Instruct v0.2's 6.5, above Koala 13B's 5.4. The paper frames this as "does not necessarily harm performance", which is a fair reading of "still a usable assistant" and a generous reading of a 1.34-point drop on a ten-point scale. The honest summary is that the robustness gain is enormous and the capability cost is real and not zero — 43.6 points of average ASR for 1.34 points of MT-Bench is an excellent trade, and it is still a trade.

The comparison also has an asymmetry the paper states plainly and which every reader should carry: the Zephyr 7B in the main tables is Zephyr 7B Beta, SFT and DPO. R2D2 is built on the SFT-only variant with no DPO stage. So the 7.34 → 6.0 gap is not purely the price of adversarial training — some of it is the missing preference-optimisation stage, which is exactly what DPO is for. The same asymmetry sits under the 69.5 → 5.5 robustness comparison in the other direction: the two rows do not differ only by R2D2. This is a preliminary demonstration of a training method, not a controlled ablation, and the paper says so. It is a caveat worth stating out loud precisely because so few defense papers state theirs.

Read the row across, not down

Now look at the sorted table again and ignore the absolute numbers. The top of that list — UAT, GCG-Transfer, GBDA, PEZ, GCG-Multi, GCG, AutoPrompt — is the entire gradient family, every method that searches over token sequences using the model's own gradients. All seven collapse, five of them below 5%. The bottom of the list — TAP, PAIR, TAP-Transfer, PAP-top5, Stochastic Few-Shot — is almost exactly the LLM-driven family, methods where another language model writes and iteratively refines a natural-language jailbreak based on what the target did last time. Those barely move: a 12.6× reduction in one column and a 1.1× reduction in another, on the same model, on the same behaviors, from the same training run.

The middle of the list is the part that tells you the split is not simply "gradient versus not". DirectRequest, Human Jailbreaks and ZeroShot also fall a long way (4.6×, 4.9×, 8.3×) despite having nothing to do with gradients. What they share is that they are non-adaptive: a fixed request, a fixed template, or a prompt written blind with no feedback from the target. Ordinary refusal training — the toward loss — handles those, and R2D2 gets that for free. What survives is the intersection: attacks that adapt to this model, in a search space R2D2 never trained against. GCG adapts, but in token space, which is where the defense lives. PAIR and TAP adapt in semantic space, and the defense has nothing to say about it.

Generalise it, because this is the lesson the whole map turns on:

An ASR number is a claim about the attack you ran, not about the model. "Zephyr + R2D2 has 5.5% ASR" and "Zephyr + R2D2 has 60.8% ASR" are both true statements about the same weights on the same 510 behaviors, graded by the same classifier, differing only in which attack produced the test cases. Anyone reporting a single robustness figure without naming the attacks that produced it is reporting a coincidence — and specifically, a coincidence about which attacks the authors happened to run, which correlates strongly with which attacks they were defending against. This is the practical content of the paper's broader finding that no model is robust to all attacks and no attack breaks all models. A robustness claim needs an attack list attached or it means nothing.

Credit where it is due: the paper says this about its own method, unprompted, in the section announcing the method's success.

"For some attacks, the improvement conferred by R2D2 is less pronounced. This is especially true for methods dissimilar to the train-time GCG adversary, including PAIR, TAP, and Stochastic Few-Shot. This suggests that incorporating multiple diverse attacks into adversarial training may be necessary to obtain generalizable robustness."— HarmBench, §6, Adversarial Training Results

That is unusual and worth saying. The normal shape of a defense paper is a table with the attacks the defense handles and a discussion section explaining why the others were out of scope. Here the weak columns are printed in the same row as the strong ones, at the same width, in the paper's main results table — which is only possible because the authors committed to a standardised battery before they built the defense. That ordering is the actual argument for benchmarks like this one: a fixed battery you cannot quietly reshape after seeing your own results.

One more caution on reading these deltas. The grader agrees with human labels about 93% of the time, so roughly 7% label noise sits under every cell. That is nowhere near enough to threaten 69.5 → 5.5. It is entirely enough to make 60.8 versus 66.5 — the TAP column — a difference you should not lean on. The read-across argument holds because the gap between the extremes is an order of magnitude, not because any individual small movement is meaningful.

The other "defenses" among the 33 targets

The paper's abstract says "33 target LLMs and defenses", which invites a misreading. R2D2 is the only defense method HarmBench implements. Everything else called a defense in that count is a shipped model that happens to have been safety-trained by its vendor. That is a meaningful category — safety training is a model-level defense — but there is no defense code in the repo for them, no configuration, nothing to reproduce. They are targets whose training pipelines happened to include refusal work.

Three groups are worth reading, all from Table 6:

  • The Llama 2 Chat family — what good refusal training looks like. 32.5 / 30.0 / 37.5 under GCG for 7B / 13B / 70B, against 0.8 / 2.8 / 2.8 under DirectRequest. That shape is the signature of RLHF refusal training done seriously: essentially nothing gets through if you simply ask, and a white-box optimizer with the weights still gets through about a third of the time. Note also that 70B is less robust than 13B — scale does not buy robustness, the training pipeline does.
  • The Claude models — the lowest numbers in the entire table. Claude 2 and Claude 2.1 sit at 2.7 and 2.6 under GCG-Transfer, under 5 on every single column they were run against, and 0.3 under Human Jailbreaks. Claude 1 is slightly higher at 12.1 under GCG-Transfer. Being API-only, the gradient methods cannot run against them at all, so the row is short.
  • The GPT models — safety-trained and clearly penetrable by transfer. GPT-4 0613 and GPT-4 Turbo 1106 hold DirectRequest to 21.0 and 9.3 and GCG-Transfer to 22.0 and 22.3, but both sit above 54 under TAP-Transfer. GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 reaches 62.3 in the same column. The transfer attacks are the ones that populate the closed-model columns at all, and they are the ones these models are worst against.
Read Claude's row twice. A near-zero row is consistent with two very different models: one that refuses harmful requests while remaining helpful on everything else, and one that refuses too much. HarmBench cannot tell them apart — it ships no benign set, no false-refusal measurement, and no capability check other than the single MT-Bench score the authors ran on their own defended model. A model that refuses every request scores 0% ASR and tops the table. Whatever Claude's numbers mean, they do not by themselves mean "well-calibrated"; that is a claim this benchmark is structurally unable to make. See the classifier's failure modes for the full shape of the blind spot.

The same asterisk applies, more gently, to R2D2 itself — and this is exactly why the MT-Bench number in Table 11 is doing more work than it looks like it is doing. It is the only evidence in the paper that any defended model on the list is still useful. Without it, "average ASR 19.1" and "refuses everything" would be indistinguishable from the results grid.

Why system-level defenses are out of scope

Defenses split into two kinds. Model-level defenses change the model: safety training, refusal mechanisms, system prompts, context distillation, adversarial training. System-level defenses wrap the model: input filters, output classifiers, prompt cleansing, perplexity checks on the incoming text, a second model reading the request before it is passed on. HarmBench evaluates the first and deliberately excludes the second, and §B.3 gives the reason:

"[W]hen assessing the robustness of a defense, it is vital to consider adaptive attacks, yet adaptive attacks for system-level defenses are highly specific to the individual defense. This makes it challenging to determine whether a defense is truly robust or simply hasn't been adequately tested (Athalye et al., 2018)."— HarmBench, §B.3

The citation is the whole argument compressed. Athalye, Carlini and Wagner (2018) took nine defenses published at a single ICLR and broke seven of them, and the common failure was not that the defenses were wrong in principle — it was that each one made gradients useless to the standard attack in a way the standard attack had not been written to handle. Run the fixed battery, get a beautiful number, publish. Write the attack that knows about the defense, and the number evaporates. The field's name for this is obfuscated gradients, and its general form is: a defense evaluated only against attacks that predate it is untested, and its score is unfalsifiable rather than good.

Now apply that to a filter. An input filter that blocks high-perplexity text will stop GCG suffixes dead, because a GCG suffix is by construction gibberish — and HarmBench's battery would score it near-perfect. The adaptive attack is obvious once you name the defense: add a fluency penalty to the search, or switch to AutoDAN, which produces readable prompts already. But that attack has to be written for that filter. A different filter — a classifier on the output rather than the input, say — needs a different adaptive attack again. There is no fixed battery that honestly evaluates an arbitrary system-level defense, because the honest evaluation is defense-specific by construction, and running the generic battery against it produces a number that measures the battery's ignorance rather than the defense's strength.

Model-level defenses do not have this property to nearly the same degree. The defense is inside the weights, the attacks in the battery already take the weights (or the API) as their input, and there is no separate component for the attack to be naive about. So a fixed battery gives you a meaningful, if incomplete, reading — and R2D2's own row is the proof that it is incomplete in a legible way: the battery caught the generalisation gap and printed it.

This is a good scoping decision, not a gap. The paper is explicit that HarmBench supports evaluating system-level defenses and simply does not do so in its own large-scale comparison. What it will not do is hand you a number for your filter and let you call it robustness. If you are evaluating a system-level defense, running the HarmBench battery against it is a useful lower bound on how bad things are and is not evidence that things are good.

What to carry away

Three things, in descending order of how often you will need them.

First, always ask which attacks. A robustness figure without an attack list is not a measurement. If the list is one attack, the figure describes that attack. If the list is one family — and "we tested GCG, GCG-Multi and GCG-Transfer" is one family — the figure describes the family. R2D2's row shows the two ends of that range differing by an order of magnitude on identical weights, and it is not an adversarially-chosen example: it is the paper's own defense, in the paper's own table.

Second, adversarial training generalises less than you hope, and further than you might fear. R2D2 did not merely memorise its 180 test cases — it transferred cleanly across the whole gradient family, including to UAT and GCG-Transfer, which it never saw, and it wiped out the non-adaptive attacks too. What it did not do is cross into a different search space. The paper's own conclusion is the right one: robustness that generalises probably requires training against diverse attacks, not one strong one.

Third, publish the capability number. Robustness is trivially achievable by refusing everything, and a benchmark that measures only ASR will reward that. The single line of Table 11 is what separates R2D2 from a model that scored well by being useless, and no comparable line exists for any of the other thirty-two targets on the list. If you build a defense, the MT-Bench row is not a courtesy — it is the half of the result that makes the other half mean anything.
