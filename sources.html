Sources, verified

What each source establishes, what it does not, and the four places where the repository and the paper do not line up.
TL;DR — Three bodies of evidence carry this map: arXiv:2402.04249v2 for the design argument and every reported number, the HarmBench repository at main for every count and every mechanism, and the artifacts that ship separately from both (three classifiers on Hugging Face, a 10.4 GB results archive on Zenodo). Counts on this site were parsed from the shipped CSVs rather than copied out of the paper — they agree, which is itself a check. Results were read from the v2 HTML tables, never from the figures. Four repo/paper mismatches are documented below with their evidence, one of which is a printed table row that cannot be reconciled with its own cells.

Every page on this site makes claims that a reader should be able to walk back to something checkable. This page is that walk-back. It is organised by artifact rather than by claim, because the useful question when you distrust a number is not “which page said that” but “what was it read out of, and does that thing actually support it.” For each source there are two columns of interest: what it establishes, and what people routinely assume it establishes but does not.

The paper

Mazeika, Phan, Yin, Zou, Wang, Mu, Sakhaee, Li, Basart, Li, Forsyth and Hendrycks, HarmBench: A Standardized Evaluation Framework for Automated Red Teaming and Robust Refusal. arXiv:2402.04249 — v1 submitted 2024-02-06, v2 2024-02-27. This map reads the v2 HTML, because the HTML renders the result grids as machine-readable tables rather than as a typeset PDF, and because v2 is the version whose numbers the community cites. Where the two versions differ in a way that matters, this site follows v2 and says so.

The paper is not one artifact but two glued together: a benchmark-design argument (sections 3 and 4, appendix B) and an empirical comparison (section 6, appendix D). The design half is where the definitions live and is where most misreadings start; the empirical half is a wall of tables. Here is which piece of it supports what.

§4.2Curation of harmful behaviors: the design principles — breadth, differential harm (prefer behaviors a search engine does not already answer), dual-intent handling, and comparability. This is why the benchmark looks the way it does.

§B.3Supported threat models: no-access (transfer), query-access and parameter-access attacks; model-level versus system-level defenses; and the explicit decision to scope the large-scale comparison to model-level defenses only.
§B.4The seven semantic categories and their sub-bullets. The taxonomy quoted on each harm page comes from here, not from the CSV, which carries only the machine slug.
§B.5.1How the Llama 2 13B evaluation classifier was trained — template-generated completions, GPT-4-0613 labels, distillation, and the separate Mistral validation classifier fine-tuned on half the data. See the grader.
§B.5.2Why copyright is graded by hashing rather than by an LLM judge: works inspired by a copyrighted original are not reliably separable from attempts to reproduce it, so the benchmark measures only near-verbatim regurgitation. Underwrites the copyright type page.

What the paper does not establish

There is no per-semantic-category ASR table. This is the single most consequential gap for anyone reading the map, and it is why six of the seven harm pages end with an honest shrug instead of a number. The category-level evidence is figures 9 and 10 and their captions: that copyright sits far below everything else, that the other six categories are roughly level on average, and that which category is easiest depends on the model family. That is all. Any per-category ranking you see quoted elsewhere with decimals attached was either read off a bar chart or invented.

Over-refusal is not measured anywhere. No benign or false-refusal set ships, and none is evaluated. A model that refuses every input scores 0% ASR and looks like the most robust system in the paper. The one capability check in the whole work is table 11’s MT-Bench column, and it covers four models — the paper’s own defended model and three comparisons — not the 33-target grid.

System-level defenses are out of scope by construction. Input filters, output classifiers, prompt cleansing and the rest of what actually sits in front of a deployed model are absent, and §B.3 says why: a fixed set of attacks cannot fairly evaluate a defense that would call for its own adaptive attack.

“For simplicity of evaluation with a fixed set of attacks, we focus our large-scale comparison on model-level defenses, although future work could use HarmBench for evaluating system-level defenses.”— HarmBench, §B.3

Read as a limitation this is fair. Read as a result it is dangerous: nothing in this benchmark tells you how a system built around one of these models behaves.

Primary papers for the red teaming methods

HarmBench implements 18 methods, but they are not 18 papers. Several table columns are variants of one method (GCG-Multi and GCG-Transfer are ensemble configurations of GCG; TAP-Transfer is TAP with a fixed attacker and judge), two are baselines HarmBench itself defines, and one entry in the grading tables is a classifier rather than an attack. Every arXiv identifier below was fetched and its title checked against the method it is claimed for. Grouped by the family pages that describe their mechanisms.

The paper is explicit that these are not always the original implementations: where a method used a closed model as its attacker or judge, HarmBench substitutes Mixtral 8x7B to make the compute comparable across the grid, and it uses the in-context version of PAP because the fine-tuned paraphraser is not public. Both substitutions can lower a method’s measured ASR relative to its own paper. Any comparison between a HarmBench column and a number from the method’s own paper has to account for that.

Where the sources disagree

Four mismatches turned up while cross-checking the repository against the paper. Three are worth knowing about; the fourth is cosmetic but tells you something about how the multimodal split was assembled. None of them is hidden — the paper flags the mechanism behind the first one itself.

1. The validation split: 40/20 in the paper, 41/19 in the CSV

§B.2 describes a 100-behavior validation set made of 20 multimodal, 20 contextual, 20 copyright and 40 standard behaviors. The shipped harmbench_behaviors_text_val.csv has 80 text rows: 41 standard, 19 contextual, 20 copyright. One behavior sits on the other side of the standard/contextual line from where the paper’s description puts it. The paper’s own account of how the split was made explains it:

“These were selected with stratified sampling using the intersections between all functional behaviors and semantic categories, followed by manual adjustment to ensure the appropriate number of behaviors in each category.”— HarmBench, §B.2

The same paragraph adds that the split was drawn against an earlier version of the semantic categories, so the per-category validation percentages do not exactly match the final taxonomy either. The practical consequence is small — one behavior out of 80 — but it is a live example of the general rule this map follows: when the CSV and the prose disagree about a count, the CSV is the benchmark. The prose describes an intent; the file is what your harness will actually load.

2. §6.3’s prose figures are not the table’s figures

Section 6.3 states R2D2’s headline result by comparing it against the Llama 2 Chat family, quoting a GCG ASR of 31.8 → 5.9. Neither number appears in the v2 tables. Table 6, the 400-behavior all-behaviors slice, gives Llama 2 7B Chat under GCG at 32.5 and Zephyr 7B + R2D2 at 5.5. Table 7, the 320-behavior test split, gives 31.9 and 6.3. The prose pair matches neither slice: it takes the low end of one and the high end of the other.

The most likely reading is that the prose survived from an earlier revision of the tables and was not refreshed for v2 — the gap is within a point in each direction and the qualitative claim is unaffected. This map uses the table figures throughout, and prefers the all-behaviors slice unless a page says otherwise. If you are reproducing R2D2 numbers, do the same, and say which slice you are quoting: the difference between 5.5 and 6.3 is smaller than the classifier’s own ~7% label noise, which is the real reason not to argue about it.

3. The Table 6 copyright “Average” row is arithmetically impossible

This is the one that matters. Table 6’s Copyright Behaviors sub-table prints an Average row beginning 50.7 41.6 36.5 … 25.6. Every individual cell in that sub-table lies between 0.0 and 28.0 — 386 cells across 30 model rows, checked. No set of values in that range averages to 50.7. Recomputing the column means from the grid gives numbers an order of magnitude smaller, and those recomputed means agree with the copyright averages the paper itself prints in tables 7 and 8:

The recomputed figures also reconcile the all-behaviors row exactly, which the printed ones cannot: weighting the three functional sub-tables by their sizes, (200×69.1 + 100×74.8 + 100×4.6) / 400 = 54.3, the printed GCG average. It works per model too — Llama 2 7B Chat under GCG, (200×34.5 + 100×58.0 + 100×3.0) / 400 = 32.5, matching its printed all-behaviors cell. Substitute the printed copyright average and nothing reconciles.

So the printed row is a typesetting error in v2, and this map reports the recomputed averages everywhere. The full arithmetic, including the weighting check for several models, is on the results page. The finding also has a substantive consequence: with the correct numbers, attack success on copyright behaviors is near the floor for every method, which is exactly what §B.5.2 predicts from a metric that only fires on near-verbatim regurgitation — and exactly what figure 9’s caption claims. The erroneous row, taken at face value, would have made copyright look like an average category and quietly contradicted the paper’s own text.

4. The multimodal tag that is never set

The smaller one. compute_results_classifier branches on a multimodal tag to decide whether to feed the classifier a RedactedImageDescription as context. The shipped multimodal CSV carries a Tags column with no values in it. The branch is therefore unreachable from the shipped data alone — multimodal evaluation runs must be tagging rows somewhere upstream of the classifier call, or supplying their own dataframe. It changes nothing about the reported results, but it is worth knowing if you are wiring your own multimodal behaviors into the pipeline: the shipped CSV will not take the branch you expect. See the multimodal page.

How this map was built

Counts were parsed, not quoted. Every number of behaviors on this site — per category, per functional type, per split, per tag — came from reading the shipped CSVs programmatically. None was copied from the paper. They match the paper’s stated totals, with the single 41/19 exception documented above. This ordering is deliberate: a benchmark is the artifact people run, and where the artifact and its description differ, the artifact wins.

Results came from the arXiv v2 HTML tables, extracted as grids and checked against each other. Where a figure and a table both bear on a claim, the table was used; where only a figure exists, the map quotes what the caption asserts and does not attempt to read values off the bars.

Per-category ASR is not available and is not invented anywhere on this site. Six of the seven harm pages state plainly that the paper does not tabulate ASR by semantic category, name the figure-level claims that touch their category, and stop there. The seventh, copyright, has real numbers because copyright is also a functional type with its own sub-table — and those are the recomputed averages, not the printed row.

What this site carries, and what it does not. The dataset is here: all 510 behaviors and their context strings are reproduced on the behavior index from the MIT-licensed repository where they are published, each linked to its own line in the source CSV, and every harm page shows its examples verbatim. The attack tooling is not: no jailbreak template, no persuasion script, no model completion, and no suffix optimised against a current model. Method pages explain mechanisms — what is searched over, what the loss is, what a step costs — and carry no payloads, with one marked exception: two 2023 GCG suffixes published by their own authors as part of a disclosure, against Vicuna and Llama 2 7B Chat, shown to make the method concrete and long since non-transferable. The other verbatim block on the site is the classifier’s judging rubric on the grader page, which is a grading instruction rather than an attack. Context passages are printed on the behavior index but not inline on the prose pages, where at up to 3,544 characters they would swamp the argument around them.

Every external URL printed on this page was checked with an HTTP request at compile time: 33 URLs, 32 returning 200, and the Zenodo record returning a traffic-based 403 to automated clients while remaining live in a browser.
