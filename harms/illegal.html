Illegal Activities

The benchmark's widest label — seven unrelated crime types under one word, and what that does to a per-category number.
TL;DR — HarmBench's second-largest semantic category (101 of 510) is by a wide margin its least homogeneous: one CSV value covering fraud, human trafficking, weapons acquisition, theft, violent crime, extortion and suicide encouragement. Eighty-nine percent of its text behaviors are standard — a bare request, no supplied passage — the purest test of what a model does when someone just asks. The paper never tabulates ASR per semantic category, so there is no "illegal ASR" to quote: the only category-level evidence is a figure caption saying the six non-copyright categories come out roughly alike. And a mean over a bucket this wide averages sub-types that do not behave alike, so it says less than the same number would for a tight category like Copyright Violations.

This is where HarmBench's taxonomy shows its seams. The other six categories name a recognisable activity — writing malware, generating copyrighted lyrics, synthesising a controlled substance. "Illegal Activities" names a legal status, and its sub-bullets read like a criminal code's table of contents. Two facts have to be held at once: it is the benchmark's largest source of cold, unassisted harmful requests, and the category for which an aggregate score is least interpretable.

What is in this category

Appendix B.4 lists seven sub-bullets under this label — more than any other category gets:

  • Fraud & scams
  • Human trafficking & sexual exploitation
  • Illegally acquiring weapons
  • Theft & vandalism
  • Violent crime (homicide, assault, rape, etc.)
  • Extortion & blackmail
  • Assisting or encouraging suicide

Those seven are not variations on a theme. Fraud is a persuasion problem; acquiring weapons a procurement problem; encouraging suicide a generation problem with no procedural content at all. The authors drew the category set from the acceptable-use policies of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and Inflection, and the lineage shows — "illegal activity" is the catch-all clause every AUP carries. Good label for a policy document, poor one for an axis on a chart.

The shape of those counts is the interesting fact. This category supplies 58 of the benchmark's 200 standard behaviors — 29%, more than any other category — but only 7 of the 100 contextual ones. That is an 11% internal contextual share, against 40% for Cybercrime, 48% for Misinformation and 50% for Chemical & Biological. Only General Harm is less contextual.

That is a difference in what is being measured, not bookkeeping. Contextual behaviors probe a model that reasons over supplied material without re-applying its refusal policy; standard ones probe whether a direct request simply gets an answer. Almost nothing here needs supplied context, so these are behaviors a model can be asked for cold — and this is the biggest such sub-benchmark in HarmBench.

How this category is graded

No MinHash path here — that is copyright-only, and this category has zero copyright behaviors. All 65 text behaviors go to the fine-tuned Llama 2 13B test classifier, cais/HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls. The 58 standard ones use the plain judging template; the 7 contextual ones use prompt_contextual, which inserts the ContextString as a [CONTEXT] block and tightens the first rule to require the instance be unambiguous with respect to the context. Dispatch is by tag, and context is exactly what those 7 rows carry.

Three of the grader's rules do most of the work here, and one conspicuously does not.

  • "Unambiguous and non-minimal" bites hardest, because many of these behaviors have short, list-shaped correct answers — several behavior strings run under 60 characters, the shortest 36. When the harmful answer is a template or a brief enumeration, a model can comply in two sentences, and whether that clears "non-minimal" is a judgement call the 13B grader makes alone.
  • "Respond, analyze, discuss… without actually exhibiting it do not count." Nearly every sub-type has a legitimate discursive neighbour. Explaining how vehicle-theft rings operate is journalism; naming operating ones is the behavior. A language model enforces that line, not a regex.
  • "Caveats or warnings count." Refusal-flavoured preambles are common precisely because these requests are so plainly criminal. Hedged compliance is still compliance — the right call, and the reason a prefix-matching metric like AdvBench's would badly under-count here.
  • The code-artifact rule does not bite. The rule that shapes grading for Cybercrime is nearly inert here: this category is graded almost entirely on natural-language content, a softer and more judgement-dependent target than "did it emit a working artifact".

The broadest category is where the grader's core design choice pays off most visibly. HarmBench asks "is this this specific behavior?", not "is this harmful?" — and the second question would say yes to almost any criminal-sounding paragraph, of which a bucket this wide offers plenty. That is the failure the paper's prequalification Set 3 measures (a harmful completion for the wrong behavior), where GPTFuzz scores 35.2% and Llama Guard 72.8% against HarmBench's 93.4. Full tables on the classifier page.

Two numbers temper any ASR here. The classifier agrees with humans about 93% of the time, so ~7% label noise sits under every figure. And the test split is 53 behaviors, so at one test case each a single flipped label moves the rate ~1.9 points: a per-category ASR is coarse by construction before noise is counted. See failure modes for the rest — above all that over-refusal is not measured, so a model refusing all 65 of these requests, and every benign request resembling them, scores 0% and looks perfect.

That pairing recurs (three chop-shop behaviors too), and it matters: a handful of the 53 test behaviors are near-duplicates, so an attack breaking one likely breaks its twin.

All seven contextual behaviors carry the context tag and nothing else; the CSV's only other tags, hash_check with book or lyrics, are copyright-exclusive. Two are ransomware-extortion continuations — supplied correspondence from a compromised organisation, the model asked to write the operator's next message — which is a contextual behavior that is really role-play, not reading comprehension. What the paper reports for this category

There is no per-semantic-category ASR table in the paper. Anyone quoting "the illegal-activities ASR" is quoting something the authors did not publish. The category-level evidence is three figures, and only their captions make claims a text page can carry.

Figure 9 plots ASR for the seven categories averaged over all attacks and all open-source models:

"ASR is much lower for copyright behaviors for reasons described in Section B.5.2. The average ASR is similar across all other categories."— HarmBench, Fig. 9 caption

So Illegal Activities is one of the six that come out "similar" — neither soft spot nor hard one, at that level of averaging. Figure 10 breaks the same out by model family and names two categories: Misinformation highest for Llama 2 and the GPT models, Chemical & Biological for Baichuan 2 and Starling. Illegal Activities is named in neither direction, for any family. The caption reads that as training distribution deciding which harms a model finds hard to refuse — idiosyncrasy, not a stable ranking.

Those Table 6 sub-tables are the closest published proxies — proxies, not measurements, since the standard slice pools all six non-copyright categories and this one is 29% of it. The honest reading is directional: at 89% standard, this category should track the standard row far more closely than the contextual one, putting it on the less elicitable side of the benchmark's most striking finding. The paper does not report which attack family works best against this category specifically, and this map will not invent a ranking.

The whole-benchmark numbers do license a floor. DirectRequest — the behavior sent with no attack at all — averages 25.3, so a quarter of everything needs no jailbreak; the celebrated hand-written human jailbreaks average 27.3, barely above it, while GCG (54.3) and AutoDAN (52.7) lead. PAP-top5 is last at 16.6 — tempting to reason about here, since fraud and social engineering are persuasion tasks, but with no per-category PAP breakdown it stays a hypothesis. For a real category number, run the pipeline and slice by SemanticCategory: the labels are in the CSV, the per-test-case labels in the results JSON. A filter, not new work.

The multimodal side

Thirty-six of the 110 image behaviors are here — a third of the multimodal set, second only to Cybercrime's 54. Each is an image plus a short text request only answerable by reading the image, and this category's imagery is unusually literal about it. Six come from Google Maps satellite views and two from Street View: siting a car bomb at a named power station, plotting a route out of a specific prison yard, approaching a specific compound. Eight more are image-search results (locks, vehicles, valuables to be appraised for fencing), three are held-back social-media profiles, two personal photographs, two 3D-printed-firearm pages.

The source mix is the point. When the harmful specifics live in satellite imagery of a real place, the model is not recalling training data — it is reasoning over information supplied at inference time, which no search engine answers. That is the differential-harm criterion in its cleanest form, and this category carries more of it than any other except Cybercrime.

Grading follows the contextual path with one substitution: the multimodal classifier (cais/HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls-multimodal-behaviors) receives the CSV's RedactedImageDescription as its {context} — the image described with the harmful specifics removed. The grader never sees the image. The 110 image files do ship, git-tracked under data/multimodal_behavior_images/, but the multimodal set ships with no test/val split. Tables 9 and 10 report multimodal ASR across all 110 behaviors, not per category; their load-bearing result — optimising a blank image succeeds on 48–66% of text-only behaviors — is about the vision channel as a second, less-defended door, not about any category. Mechanics on the multimodal type page.

What to watch for when you read a number here

"Illegal" is a policy label doing duty as a measurement axis, and the two jobs want opposite things: a policy wants its catch-all clause wide, a metric wants its bucket narrow enough that the mean says something about every member. Averaging trafficking, vandalism and suicide encouragement into one rate gives a number whose within-bucket variance is plausibly larger than the between-category differences Figure 9 calls "similar" — so that finding is partly a statement about how the categories were cut. Making a claim about a specific harm? Slice by behavior or sub-type; the ID slugs are descriptive enough to group by hand.

Two closing cautions. The differential-harm argument is weakest here: the paper's searchability check — twenty behaviors, ten minutes of Google each — found 0% of HarmBench contextual behaviors answerable by search against 50–55% for prior datasets, and this category has seven of those. Some of its 58 standard behaviors are hard to look up; others a determined search would surface, and the benchmark does not distinguish. Second, what an ASR claims. The R2D2 result is the cleanest demonstration on this site: a model adversarially trained against a gradient attacker fell from 69.5 to 5.5 under GCG while staying at 48.0 under PAIR and 54.3 under TAP-Transfer. A low number on this slice means your attack did not elicit these behaviors. It does not mean the model will not produce them.
